Egg donation in India, in plain words

Who can donate, what the cycle involves, what a donor gets, and what the law says. Written for agencies, donors and the families they work with.

Who can be an egg donor

  • An ever-married woman — that is, a woman who has been married at least once
  • Aged 23 to 35 years
  • Has at least one living child of her own, and that child is at least three years old
  • Medically and psychologically fit, with screening completed
  • Not a relative of the couple in the usual case — donation under the Act is anonymous

These come from the Assisted Reproductive Technology (Regulation) Act, 2021. The Act also limits how often a woman may donate and how many eggs are collected in a cycle — practice varies, so confirm the current position with the bank.

What a donor gets

  • Paid. Unlike surrogacy, egg donation is compensated. The Act allows reasonable compensation for her time, effort and expenses.
  • Insurance cover for the donor, arranged by the commissioning couple.
  • All medical costs of the cycle — scans, bloods, medicines, injections, retrieval.
  • Care at a registered ART clinic or bank, with proper records.

Screening before donation

Every donor is screened before she starts. Expect most of these:

  • Blood group, haemoglobin, complete blood count
  • HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, syphilis
  • Hormones — AMH, FSH, thyroid, prolactin
  • Ultrasound scan and antral follicle count
  • Genetic screening such as karyotype or thalassaemia where indicated
  • A psychological assessment

The cycle, step by step

  • Day 2–3 of her periodBaseline scan and blood tests. If all is well, stimulation begins.
  • Days 3 to 12Daily hormone injections, usually 8 to 12 days. She can give them herself at home after a demonstration.
  • Every 2 to 3 daysShort scans and bloods to track how the follicles are growing. Dose may be adjusted.
  • Trigger injectionOne final injection timed to the hour, about 34 to 36 hours before retrieval.
  • Retrieval dayA short procedure under light sedation, about 15 to 20 minutes. She goes home the same day.
  • AfterA day or two of rest. Mild cramping and light spotting are normal. Her part is over — the eggs go to the lab.

Anonymity and consent

  • The couple does not learn the donor's identity, and the donor does not learn theirs.
  • The bank shares a non-identifying profile: height, complexion, blood group, education, broad medical history.
  • She gives written consent, explained in a language she understands.
  • The bank keeps the records. Details also go to the national registry.

Common questions

Is the retrieval painful?

It is done under light sedation, so most donors feel nothing during it. Afterwards there can be mild cramping for a day or two, like period pain.

How long does the whole thing take?

About two weeks of injections, plus the retrieval day. Monitoring visits are short.

Can she keep working?

Yes. The scans are quick and injections are at home. Most donors take one day off for the retrieval.

Will it affect her own fertility?

No. The eggs collected are the ones her body was going to lose that month anyway.

Can she donate more than once?

The Act limits repeat donation. Ask the bank what the current position is before promising a donor a second cycle.

Does she meet the couple?

Usually not. The match is made on the profile sheet, not on meeting.

For agencies: the three conditions that most often disqualify a donor are the ever-married requirement, the age band, and the three-year-old child. Screen for those three before anything else.
